## Abstract

Conserving and restoring endangered corals requires colonies to survive, grow, and retain living tissue. For the elkhorn coral *Acropora palmata*, evidence on these processes is scattered among natural-reef monitoring programs and restoration experiments conducted under different environmental conditions. We synthesized longitudinal observations from 18 Caribbean studies to evaluate size-dependent survival, growth, tissue loss, and movement among five size classes. Larger colonies survived more often, but proportional growth declined with size, and surviving colonies frequently lost tissue. In a model without sexual recruitment that included fragmentation, persistence of the largest colonies contributed most to population growth; the deterministic $\lambda$ was 0.953, indicating declining colony abundance. Survival varied widely among studies, and differences in propagation history, handling, and study setting prevented a ranking of outplanting practices. Simulated mortality matching the 2023 Florida heatwave further reduced effective $\lambda$ to 0.850 even when events recurred only once every 50 years. The synthesis identifies survival and retained live area as complementary measures of planted cohorts. Establishing whether restoration rebuilds self-sustaining populations and reef habitat also requires observations of sexual recruitment and reef structure.

Keywords: Caribbean coral reefs; demographic synthesis; elkhorn coral; meta-analysis; population dynamics; restoration

## Introduction

Restoring endangered reef-building corals requires knowing which colonies survive after planting and whether they grow enough to replace lost living cover. Caribbean acroporid declines have reduced coral cover and the habitat once provided by *Acropora palmata* [@aronsonprecht2001; @gardner2003]. Disease, storms, predation, and chronic tissue loss alter the abundance and condition of remaining colonies [@gladfelter1982; @rogersmuller2012; @williamsmiller2012]. Restoration programs propagate and outplant *A. palmata*, but rebuilding populations requires more than adding colonies: planted corals must survive, grow, and retain live tissue [@edmundsriegl2020]. Population rehabilitation also differs from recovery of ecosystem structure and function [@croquer2026]. Evidence on colony demography comes from both restoration experiments and monitoring of natural reefs, scattered among regions and disturbance histories. Bringing those observations together could identify recurring demographic constraints and show which restoration decisions still lack comparable evidence.

Colony size is one restoration choice for which demographic evidence is directly relevant. Practitioners also choose propagation material, nursery treatment, attachment, and planting sites; these choices differ in their effects on early survival and later performance [@forrester2012; @williams2023; @ramosromero2025]. Large colonies can survive, reproduce sexually, and produce fragments, whereas small corals can grow rapidly in proportional terms but experience high mortality [@highsmith1982; @hughes1984; @hughesjackson1985; @lirman2000; @madin2014]. This contrast makes size at outplanting a potential trade-off between retaining colonies and increasing their area. Survival alone does not resolve that trade-off. Storm damage, disease, and predation can remove live tissue from survivors and move them into smaller classes [@lirman1997; @madin2020]. Partial mortality can also affect reproductive output: *A. palmata* colonies with unhealed lesions produced smaller eggs than apparently healthy colonies [@pinongonzalez2018]. A synthesis of size-dependent survival, growth, and tissue loss can therefore evaluate whether the evidence supports a common size-related pattern, rather than treating survival as a complete measure of recovery.

Survival and growth rates also depend on the conditions experienced during monitoring. Storm frequency and intensity alter population trajectories in stage-structured simulations [@lirman2003], while field observations and larval experiments document effects of disturbance at different life stages [@randall2009; @rogersmuller2012]. Disease outbreaks can cluster within local populations [@lentz2011]. Consequently, differences among studies may reflect exposure history as well as colony size. Excluding disturbed intervals would remove much of the environment in which restoration operates, but pooling them without retaining exposure information could conceal the conditions associated with survival and growth. The 2023 marine heatwave, which caused functional extinction of *A. palmata* in Florida [@manzello2025], illustrates the consequence. Survival measured before the event omits its mortality pulse; projections using those earlier rates alone cannot represent the loss caused by its recurrence.

Synthesis is useful only if it preserves these differences among studies. Naturally occurring fragments, nursery-reared material, and sexually propagated juveniles differ in initial size, handling, and environment [@forrester2012; @banister2024; @ramosromero2025]. Early survival and later performance can respond to different processes [@williams2008; @williams2023]. A pooled estimate for “restoration fragments” may therefore describe the mix of propagation histories and observation settings sampled rather than the effect of a particular practice. Combining studies can quantify the range of observed survival and reveal how the mix of those groups affects the estimate. It cannot rank interventions when treatment, site, and colony size vary together. That distinction defines what the existing evidence can contribute to husbandry and outplanting decisions.

Demographic models connect survival, changes in colony size, and fragment production to changes in colony abundance. Previous models evaluated these processes in *Acropora cervicornis* in Puerto Rico [@mercadomolina2015] and *A. palmata* in the northern Florida Keys [@vardi2012]. We combined fate-tracking records of individual colonies from seven studies with demographic summaries from eleven additional studies to address three linked questions: how survival, growth, and tissue loss varied with colony size; how much annual survival varied among studies and propagation or observation settings; and which size-class transitions contributed most to changes in colony number in a model without sexual recruitment. We retained disturbance histories to describe the conditions underlying the observations, then applied Florida heatwave mortality as a separate projection scenario. This approach evaluates demographic patterns that can inform monitoring and outplant-size trials without attributing differences among unrelated studies to restoration treatments.

## Materials and methods

### Literature search and study selection

From June through December 2025, we searched repositories maintained by NOAA and the US Geological Survey, structured Google Scholar results, backward and forward citations from focal demographic studies, and data shared by restoration practitioners. In March 2026, we expanded the search through PubMed, Web of Science, Semantic Scholar/Elicit, targeted citation searches, and the project literature library. Search terms combined *Acropora palmata* or elkhorn coral with survival, mortality, growth, recruitment, restoration, and long-term monitoring. Additional individual colony records were obtained by direct data sharing in April 2026. We assembled a public data and analysis repository containing the search strings, screening decisions, and study-level exclusions (<https://github.com/stier-lab/fundemar-rse-parameters>; Online Resource 1, Table S1).

We included studies that reported longitudinal observations of identified *A. palmata* colonies or fragments, survival or areal growth over a defined interval, and sufficient information to annualize survival or estimate a size-dependent vital rate. We excluded cross-sectional condition surveys, studies that reported only branch extension, laboratory micro-fragment experiments smaller than 1 cm², and overlapping analyses of the same tagged-colony datasets. Titles and abstracts were screened first, followed by full-text evaluation. A second reviewer independently evaluated a subset of expanded-search candidates; disagreements underwent a third review. Across all sources, we identified approximately 2,500 records, assessed 101 distinct study or data sources at full text or repository level, and excluded 83 with documented reasons; the most common reasons were a different species, overlap with an included dataset, and cross-sectional designs. The screening ledger contained 104 rows; we consolidated one duplicate Gladfelter record and three Vardi entries representing the same underlying study. When a source supplied an eligible parameter but another parameter was excluded, we counted the study once as included.

The final dataset comprised 18 studies. Seven supplied individual colony records: NOAA long-term monitoring of tagged colonies in the Florida Keys, Curaçao, and Navassa; Neely et al. [-@neely2022]; Pausch et al. [-@pausch2018]; Kuffner et al. [-@kuffner2020]; a USGS outplant experiment in the US Virgin Islands; FUNDEMAR nursery fragments in the Dominican Republic; and Mendoza Quiroz et al. [-@mendozaquiroz2023]. Eleven supplied study-level summaries [@rogers1982; @bruckner2001; @ortizprosper2005; @garrison2008; @williamsmiller2010; @vardi2011; @rogersmuller2012; @forrester2013; @maurer2022; @rosales2024; @ramosromero2025]. Seventeen of these studies, contributing 22 study-region effects, entered the survival synthesis. Mendoza Quiroz et al. [-@mendozaquiroz2023] contributed only to the size-dependent analyses (Online Resource 1, Table S1). Online Resource 1 summarizes the verified selection counts; early identification totals were approximate and could not support a fully enumerated PRISMA flow.

### Data extraction and standardization

We classified records into two tiers. Tier 1 studies supplied colony identifiers and repeated observations of colony size and fate; these records supported analyses of survival, growth, tissue loss, and size-class transitions. Tier 2 studies supplied study-level survival proportions, sample sizes, and, where available, mean colony size. Collector verification removed two Neely colonies belonging to another species and supplied a previously missing survey date. The corrected standardized survival file contained 7,880 interval records. Deduplication by study, colony identifier, survey year, location, and source interval identifier where available removed 66 records; removing 36 records with missing survival or missing or non-positive starting size left 7,778 inventory records. Of these, 94 endpoint-selected natural-fragment records and 21 intervals without verified duration were excluded from survival models, leaving 7,663 eligible intervals. The growth file contained 6,319 records; removing 19 duplicates left 6,300 prepared records. These counts describe colony intervals, which can include repeated observations of the same colony.

We expressed colony size in cm² using each source's area measurement or area index. Where perpendicular dimensions and live-tissue proportion were available, live area was length × width × proportion live tissue, following the convention used in previous *A. palmata* size-structured models [@vardi2012]. Neely et al. [-@neely2022] instead supplied a live-area index, $\mathrm{LAI}=[(L+W+H)/3]^2\times p_{\mathrm{live}}$, where $L$, $W$, and $H$ are colony dimensions and $p_{\mathrm{live}}$ is the fraction of living tissue. We retained that index as a size proxy; it is not a photo-traced planar area. For Mendoza Quiroz's diameter-only records, we multiplied maximum diameter squared by the mean width-to-length ratio from that study's paired measurements. Single-linear-measure conversions therefore used estimated shapes, rather than assuming a circular footprint. We refer to these comparable but nonidentical measurements collectively as area. Size-dependent analyses required a measured starting size, and growth required measurements at both endpoints. Known survival without a measured size was not coded as zero area, and missing survey dates were not imputed. Online Resource 1, section 1, gives the source-record checks and missing-date inventory.

We annualized non-annual survival as $S_{\mathrm{annual}} = S_{\mathrm{observed}}^{1/t}$, where $t$ is the monitoring interval in years. This transformation assumes a constant hazard within each interval and therefore does not represent seasonal mortality peaks. Mortality definitions also differed among studies, from loss of both tissue and skeleton in NOAA monitoring to loss of at least half of live tissue in Kuffner et al. [-@kuffner2020]. We retained each study's definition rather than imposing a common one, so part of the among-study variation reflects measurement rather than biology. The Neely index can also combine several tissue isolates on an older skeleton; equal index values need not indicate equal age, intact colony size, or reproductive condition. Known clonality at Dry Tortugas and Marker 3, and suspected clonality elsewhere, further means that colony counts cannot be interpreted as numbers of independent genotypes [@groberdunsmore2007].

We assigned colonies to five size classes (SCs): SC1, 0 < area ≤ 10 cm²; SC2, 10 < area ≤ 100 cm²; SC3, 100 < area ≤ 900 cm²; SC4, 900 < area ≤ 4,000 cm²; and SC5, area > 4,000 cm². We retained the 100, 900, and 4,000 cm² boundaries of the published *A. palmata* model to preserve comparability [@vardi2012], and divided its smallest class at 10 cm² to distinguish the smallest colonies. Vardi et al. linked the larger boundaries to branch development and increasing clonal and sexual reproductive potential. Our classes describe live-area ranges, not verified life stages or reproductive states: a small colony may be a recruit, a fragment, or a larger colony that lost tissue. We called any negative annual change in a surviving colony's measured live area tissue loss. Following the size-class transitions in Vardi et al. [-@vardi2012], we used shrinkage for movement into a smaller size class; retrogression is the equivalent matrix term used in the figures. Tissue loss could occur without shrinkage across a class boundary. Stasis meant survival in the same size class, despite possible gains or losses of tissue.

We calculated relative growth rate as $\mathrm{RGR} = G/A_0$, where $G$ is annual areal change (cm² yr^−1^) and $A_0$ is initial colony area (cm²), giving RGR in yr^−1^. We used live-tissue area and live-tissue change where available, with total-colony measurements as fallbacks. Negative RGR indicates a decrease in measured area; positive RGR indicates an increase.

### Disturbance and restoration classification

We retained all eligible monitoring intervals in the primary analyses, including those spanning disturbances. For an exploratory comparison, we assembled an 18-entry catalogue of storms, disease, bleaching, cold events, and chronic pressures, then assigned regional calendar-year screening windows (Online Resource 1, Table S2). The catalogue records the supporting sources, window boundaries, duration labels, and our analytical classification for every entry. Some windows represent documented events; others extend a reported chronic or indirect pressure across years selected for contextual screening. They do not establish continuous exposure at every reef within that region.

We matched each observation interval to catalogue entries by region and inclusive calendar-year overlap. Caribbean-wide entries applied to every represented region. We assigned three mutually exclusive states: no curated overlap when no entry matched; context-only pressure when one or more entries matched but none was flagged for exclusion; and acute-event overlap when at least one matched entry carried our acute-event exclusion flag. The flag was an analytical choice, separate from duration or biological mechanism: the prolonged 2005 thermal anomaly was flagged, whereas the 2014–2017 bleaching regime remained contextual. The acute-event sensitivity omitted flagged intervals; the primary analyses retained them. Regional labels and long screening windows could associate a colony record with an event or pressure not observed at its site. The matching algorithm, worked examples, and study-window counts are in Online Resource 1, section 2 and Table S3. We tested size-by-state associations in natural colonies as descriptive comparisons, without treating the states as measured colony exposure or estimating event-specific mortality.

For the separate comparison of fragment and juvenile records, we defined seven descriptive groups by propagation history, handling, and observation setting: (1) naturally occurring fragments (NOAA monitoring); (2) nursery outplants (Pausch et al. [-@pausch2018]); (3) block-mounted nursery fragments (Kuffner et al. [-@kuffner2020]); (4) reef outplants of unspecified origin (USGS experiment); (5) nursery-held fragments (FUNDEMAR); (6) sexually propagated reef outplants (Mendoza Quiroz et al. [-@mendozaquiroz2023], Cuevones and Picudas); and (7) sexually propagated nursery juveniles (the same study, Puerto Morelos). Kuffner's fragments were nursery-reared, epoxied to plastic discs, and attached by removable bolts to blocks on the reef; this was not a test of reattaching colonies broken after planting. The USGS records did not establish whether the material came directly from wild colonies or through a nursery. FUNDEMAR fragments remained on nursery tables.

We classified population origin independently of fragmentation. Naturally attached fragments remained natural-origin material, whereas nursery-held material and reef outplants retained their restoration provenance. Neely's [-@neely2022] shared date and live-area extract did not include colony-type codes, so we recorded fragment status as unknown and did not classify those colonies as intact or assign them to a fragment subgroup. Only NOAA records explicitly flagged as fragments entered the documented naturally occurring fragment group, not the full NOAA dataset or Vardi's [-@vardi2011] separate fragmentation-production estimates. The NOAA flag was assigned among colonies alive at the observation endpoint. These selected records could describe growth of surviving fragments but could not estimate survival from the number present at the start of monitoring. We withheld their survival estimate and excluded them from all fitted survival inputs; their growth retained its natural origin. The seven groups retain study and setting differences; they are not seven independently replicated restoration treatments (Online Resource 1, Fig. S5).

### Size-dependent vital rates

To evaluate size-related patterns, we fitted binomial generalized additive models (GAMs) for survival and Gaussian GAMs for relative growth, using log-transformed colony size as the predictor. The survival curve used natural colonies after excluding subannual intervals; the relative-growth curve used all colony sources, with the live-area-preferred and total-area fallback rule described above. Points and fitted curves in each panel came from the same model population. These descriptive curves pooled observations without adjustment for study. We evaluated sensitivity to study differences with mixed-effect models including a study-level random intercept; additional diagnostic models included site, year, or colony effects where supported by the data. Following Detmer et al. [-@detmer2025], we first compared each GAM with a linear model. The threshold gate required an improvement of at least 2 AIC units (AICc for samples smaller than 40); all primary curves used AIC. Derivatives then located changes in slope or curvature. A second-derivative minimum identifies strongest downward curvature, not a mathematical inflection, which would require a change in the sign of curvature. These features of fitted curves were not tested minimum sizes for outplanting. We checked model fit and repeated the analyses with each study excluded in turn to assess their dependence on study composition.

### Survival synthesis

To summarize annual survival and its variation among studies, we aggregated annualized survival proportions into study-region effects. For Tier 1 data, we estimated survival within groups sharing study, region, population type, and interval duration, annualized those proportions, and averaged them within effects using the number of interval records as weights. This synthesis used 7,611 Tier 1 records from the inventory: 52 Mendoza Quiroz et al. [-@mendozaquiroz2023] records were outside the survival-synthesis study set, 21 Neely et al. [-@neely2022] records lacked survey dates needed for duration, and 94 endpoint-selected NOAA fragment records were excluded from the natural-colony NOAA effects [@williamsmiller2016data]. Tier 2 effects contributed a further denominator of 1,431, giving 9,042 across 22 effects. Within the hand-extracted Tier 2 summaries, we used the maximum initial sample size within each effect rather than summing repeated census denominators, and weighted annualized survival by initial sample size. The total denominator therefore combines Tier 1 colony-interval records and Tier 2 effect-level sample sizes; it is not a count of unique colonies. The broad restoration-origin group includes nursery-held material as well as reef outplants; it is not a pooled estimate of post-outplant survival alone.

We transformed survival proportions to log odds, which compare survival with mortality on an unbounded scale. When an effect contained no survivors or no deaths, we added 0.5 to both counts so that the log odds and sampling variance remained finite. The primary random-effects model used restricted maximum likelihood (REML), with study and within-study effect structure and *t*-based inference; an independent-effect model with Knapp–Hartung inference was a sensitivity analysis [@knapp2003; @viechtbauer2010]. We report pooled survival, 95% confidence intervals, prediction intervals, and heterogeneity statistics. We evaluated population type, colony size, year, and region as exploratory moderators because the number of effects was limited. We also repeated the synthesis after excluding individual effects, changing classification assumptions, and restricting studies by methodological quality. We assessed small-study asymmetry with Egger-type regression tests on both models. A trim-and-fill sensitivity analysis added hypothetical effects to make the independent-effect funnel plot more symmetric and recalculated pooled survival. These hypothetical effects were not additional observations. Funnel-plot methods were designed for comparative effect sizes, so we treated them as diagnostics of asymmetry among single-proportion effects rather than direct tests of publication bias.

For the separate colony-source comparison, we displayed study–region summaries and record-weighted subtype means without uncertainty bars in either panel. Growth used only records with measured initial live area and live-area change, without total-area substitution. The small number of studies per subtype could not support comparable between-program uncertainty for survival and growth; variation among plotted studies remained descriptive. The endpoint-selected NOAA fragment subset was excluded from cohort-survival comparisons.

### Transition model

To connect individual rates to colony replacement, we constructed a five-class Lefkovitch matrix describing annual changes in the abundance of natural colonies. The matrix included survival, growth, shrinkage to smaller classes, and fragment production; sexual recruitment was fixed at zero. Its dominant eigenvalue ($\lambda$) therefore described annual growth of this recruitment-free model, not growth of the full population. Four studies contributed survival rates and two contributed growth rates. NOAA monitoring [@williamsmiller2016data] supplied most SC5 survival observations, and Vardi [-@vardi2011] supplied the only fragmentation estimates. We calculated matrix-cell elasticities: the proportional change in $\lambda$ expected from a small proportional change in each cell, with other cells held constant. To propagate sampling uncertainty, we resampled studies, then observations within sampled studies. We retained 2,000 valid bootstrap replicates; 124 imputed survival for at least one missing size class. The bootstrap did not propagate survival–growth covariance. We also excluded each of the four contributing studies in turn, refitting the same survival estimator and growth transitions. Excluding Vardi [-@vardi2011] also removed its fragmentation estimates; missing survival classes used the full-data estimates.

For transitions conditional on survival, we required measured initial live area or the source live-area index and its annual change, without substituting total-colony area. We excluded records whose observed live endpoint was zero, so that deaths entered the survival estimates rather than the growth transitions. We restricted natural-colony records to 0.5–1.5-year intervals, removed biologically impossible growth and the upper 1% of absolute live-area changes, and retained 4,791 records. We projected one-year area as initial live area plus annual live-area change, assigning projected areas below 0.1 cm² to 0.1 cm². This linear annualization approximated a common interval, rather than directly observing every colony after exactly one year; a positive observed endpoint could project below zero and receive the floor.

To test whether independent survival and growth resampling affected uncertainty, we compared two additional 2,000-replicate bootstraps. Both retained two draws from the studies shared by survival and growth and two from survival-only studies; the coordinated version used the same shared-study draws for survival and growth. This comparison tested study-level coordination while holding the study mix fixed, but could not estimate temporal survival–growth covariance. We also replaced missing-class survival with a draw from its full-data bootstrap distribution, compared this with point imputation and discarding incomplete replicates, and removed the survival and fragmentation contributions of Vardi [-@vardi2011] separately (Online Resource 1, Table S4).

### Heatwave scenarios

We applied the 2023 Florida heatwave mortality as an additional episodic survival pulse to the pooled reference matrix. The monitoring records extend through 2024; this matrix was not fitted as a pre-heatwave counterfactual, and the scenarios do not reconstruct the historical event. We first used the *A. palmata* dose-response thresholds reported by Manzello et al. [-@manzello2025] (ED50 = 7.8 degree-heating weeks [DHW], ED95 = 17.6 DHW) to define 8-, 12-, and 18-DHW sensitivity scenarios. We then added an observed-2023 Florida endpoint: 97.8--100% mortality, represented by a 0--2.2% heatwave-year survival multiplier. The deterministic projection used the midpoint of that range; because the published study reports bounds rather than a colony-level sampling distribution, Monte Carlo projections sampled uniformly across the reported range at each prescribed event. Each of 500 simulations per scenario retained the full-data matrix structure, scaled by one sampled bootstrap lambda for all 50 years; these simulations propagated parameter uncertainty, not environmental variation in annual transition rates. Heatwave pulses were applied after the annual matrix transition to all size classes, consistent with the reported absence of size refuge during the Florida event. We report the geometric mean of annual abundance ratios as effective lambda under recurrence intervals of 5, 10, 20, and 50 years, and the fraction of simulations in which total abundance fell below 10% of its initial value at year 50 (quasi-extinction). We prescribed these recurrence intervals to compare frequent and rare events; we did not estimate future return periods. The projections describe the recruitment-free subsystem under additional Florida-like mortality and cannot estimate Caribbean-wide thermal mortality or full population viability.

To test sensitivity to matrix structure and initial size composition, we projected all 2,000 complete bootstrap matrices and compared these with matrices scaled by the corresponding bootstrap lambdas. Each matrix remained fixed for 50 years, and heatwave mortality draws were identical across paired comparisons. The original starting population contained 100, 50, 30, 20, and 10 colonies in SC1–SC5; alternative starting proportions were 70, 15, 8, 5, and 2% (small-colony-heavy), their exact reverse of 2, 5, 8, 15, and 70% (large-colony-heavy), and the full-data matrix’s stable size distribution. These compositions were sensitivity assumptions, not estimates of Caribbean population structure (Online Resource 1, Table S5).

### Reproducibility

We conducted analyses in R 4.5.2 under the project’s locked computational environment; the central workflow uses `mgcv` for generalized additive models, `metafor` for meta-analysis, and `lme4` for mixed-effect diagnostic models. The public repository contains the data-processing and analysis scripts, figures, and tables, with links from each main-text result to its generating script and output (Data availability statement below). A versioned archival release will be deposited upon acceptance.

## Results

### Evidence spanned the Caribbean but remained geographically uneven

Demographic records covered 13 Caribbean regions, but the Florida Keys supplied 6,654 observations (85.5% of the 7,778 prepared individual-level survival records), more than all other regions combined (Fig. 1). Several regions contributed records from only a subset of size classes. Records of the largest colonies were concentrated in Florida, Curaçao, and Navassa, leaving their survival and transitions poorly documented elsewhere.

### Survival increased with size while proportional growth declined

Survival generally increased with colony size, although size explained only 8.1% of deviance in individual fate among the 5,688 fitted natural-colony records (Fig. 2a). The survival GAM fit better than a linear model (ΔAIC = 9.6), with strongest downward curvature near 7,498 cm²; the estimated colony size at this curvature minimum depended strongly on which study was retained. Survival therefore increased gradually rather than identifying a stable cutoff between low- and high-survival colonies. Relative growth declined with size across 4,998 records from all colony sources, with strongest downward curvature near 35 cm² (2.5th–97.5th percentiles across leave-one-study-out fits: 33.3–38.7 cm²). The positive-growth model did not pass the nonlinearity gate, so we did not report a corresponding threshold (Online Resource 1, Fig. S1). Neither fitted curvature nor the pooled survival pattern established an experimentally tested outplant size. Absolute-growth variability increased strongly with size, while proportional changes were less variable in large colonies.

Study-level survival estimates by size class showed that the size effect was concentrated in the largest classes (Fig. 2c). Pooled survival was nearly flat from SC1 to SC3 (77.9%, 75.5%, and 77.0%) and then rose to 91.1% in SC4 and 94.9% in SC5. Among-study spread was widest in SC1–SC3, where study context, disturbance history, and population type also varied most strongly. Colony size alone did not account for the wide variation among studies.

### Annual survival varied widely among Caribbean studies

The expanded synthesis estimated pooled annual survival at 78.9% (95% CI: 70.7--85.2%; 95% prediction interval: 38.0--95.8%) across 17 studies and 22 study-region effects (Fig. 3a). Effects calculated from individual colony records (Tier 1) and published study summaries (Tier 2) were both annualized before aggregation. Heterogeneity was high (I² = 97.6%; $\tau^2$ = 0.71, of which 0.28 was between studies and 0.43 within studies), so the pooled value summarizes the assembled evidence rather than a Caribbean-wide constant. Excluding any one parent study changed the primary estimate by at most 1.6 percentage points; excluding individual effects changed the independent-effect estimate by at most 2.5 points. Restricting the independent-effect synthesis to the 13 studies with the highest methodological-quality scores changed it by less than 1 point. Evidence for small-study asymmetry was mixed: the Egger-type test was not significant for the independent-effect model (*p* = 0.71) but was significant for the three-level model (*p* = 0.001). Adding five hypothetical effects to make the independent-effect funnel plot more symmetric lowered its estimate from 80.3% to 74.8%; those effects were not observed studies.

Pooled survival was 85.7% for natural-colony effects (95% CI 73.6–92.8%, *k* = 10) and 74.5% for restoration-origin effects (95% CI 63.9–82.9%, *k* = 12). The natural-colony estimate was 11.2 percentage points higher, calculated before rounding the group percentages, but uncertainty was too large to distinguish the groups in the moderator test (*p* = 0.16). With 22 effects, the estimated difference needed for 80% power was about 20 percentage points, so the observed difference could remain unresolved even if real. The analysis therefore supported neither a reliable ranking nor a claim of equal survival. Regional variation remained substantial within population types (Fig. 3b). Even where the two types overlapped in colony size, study identity and sampling context could not be separated from population origin (Online Resource 1, Fig. S2).

### Surviving natural colonies often lost tissue

Surviving natural colonies lost live tissue in 39.5% of 4,791 near-annual growth records from two studies (Online Resource 1, Fig. S3). Tissue-loss frequency increased from 22.5% in SC1 to 44.4% in SC5. In the same records, annualized downward-class probabilities were 5.4% in SC2, 14.2% in SC3, 17.4% in SC4, and 11.4% in SC5. Movement into a larger class occurred in 47.5% of SC1 records (*n* = 40), 27.0% of SC2 (*n* = 644), 13.6% of SC3 (*n* = 1,416), and 9.8% of SC4 (*n* = 1,308). SC5 had no higher modeled class, although colonies could gain area within it. These transitions were conditional on survival and projected to one year from observed intervals. Tissue loss was more frequent than movement into a smaller class because colonies could lose area without crossing a class boundary.

### Size--survival associations differed across regional screening categories

Of 1,003 monitoring-window groups, 96.5% matched at least one regional screening window (Online Resource 1, Tables S2 and S3). This percentage describes catalogue overlap, not measured exposure in 96.5% of colonies. Size--survival relationships differed among the three screening categories in natural colonies (global likelihood-ratio test, *p* = 0.004; Online Resource 1, Fig. S4); the size-by-category interaction was also supported for positive growth (*p* = 0.003). The cell percentages in Fig. S4 are descriptive, not pairwise tests of SC4 or SC5 survival across states. In particular, the no-curated-overlap group represented a restricted set of studies and years and included only one SC1 survival record, with no corresponding growth records. Removing baseline-exclusion intervals changed both study coverage and pooled survival in the individual-record sensitivity analysis. These comparisons did not isolate mortality caused by the events or demonstrate a survival benefit of disturbance.

### Restoration survival varied among colony-source subtypes

The seven-group inventory contained 1,257 survival records from six studies, of which 1,163 could describe cohort survival after withholding 94 endpoint-selected naturally occurring fragments (NOAA monitoring; Online Resource 1, Fig. S5). Record-weighted survival was 57.4% for nursery outplants (Pausch, *n* = 968), 81.1% for block-mounted nursery fragments (Kuffner, *n* = 53), 65.2% for reef outplants of unspecified origin (USGS, *n* = 46), and 86.4% for nursery-held fragments (FUNDEMAR, *n* = 44). Sexually propagated reef outplants (*n* = 35) and nursery juveniles (*n* = 17) in Mendoza Quiroz had no observed deaths. These were descriptive means from different sites and cohorts, not estimates of propagation or handling effects. Only four studies supplied the paired live-area measurements required for the growth panel (*n* = 857); missing growth estimates were not treated as zero.

### Largest-class stasis dominated conditional transition dynamics

The recruitment-free transition matrix estimated a deterministic lambda of 0.953 (95% bootstrap interval: 0.913–1.000; Fig. 4). The upper limit was just above replacement (1.0001 before three-decimal rounding). In 97.4% of hierarchical-bootstrap replicates, lambda was below one. This resampling fraction characterizes uncertainty in the compiled transition rates; it is not a probability of real-world population decline. Stasis dominated the transition matrix, and SC5-to-SC5 stasis accounted for 51.5% of matrix-cell elasticity (95% bootstrap interval: 38.8–64.7%). Leave-one-study-out estimates of lambda ranged from 0.888 (excluding Vardi [-@vardi2011], including its fragmentation estimates) to 0.966 (excluding NOAA monitoring [@williamsmiller2016data]). Excluding Neely et al. [-@neely2022] gave lambda of 0.954. These exclusions used the same survival estimator and transition filters as the primary matrix. Removing fragmentation altogether lowered lambda to 0.889, so asexual fragment production, estimated from a single study, contributed about 0.064 to the deterministic growth rate. Within this conditional, recruitment-free subsystem, persistence of the largest colonies had more influence on lambda than marginal changes in any single early transition.

Most bootstrap estimates remained below replacement under alternative resampling choices: 99.3% of lambda estimates were below one with coordinated shared-study draws (95% interval: 0.921–0.989), compared with 97.6% under independent resampling with the same study mix (Online Resource 1, Table S4). Lambda was below one in 97.6% of the 2,000 replicates under both point and distribution-based missing-class imputation; their upper interval bound was 0.9990, just below replacement. Removing Vardi’s [-@vardi2011] survival data alone lowered lambda to 0.951, whereas removing its fragmentation estimates alone lowered it to 0.889. The combined exclusion effect therefore arose mainly from fragment production.

### Florida-scale heatwaves reduced effective lambda

Adding heatwave mortality reduced projected abundance without changing the underlying pooled reference matrix. Under the observed 2023 Florida endpoint, effective lambda was 0.356, 0.577, 0.770, and 0.850 when a Florida-like event recurred every 5, 10, 20, and 50 years, respectively (Online Resource 1, Fig. S6). In these primary simulations, every observed-endpoint scenario was below the quasi-extinction threshold at year 50 in all 500 simulations. Without heatwaves, 83.8% of the 500 primary simulations were below the year-50 threshold, and effective lambda was 0.936. This finite-horizon rate differed from the deterministic eigenvalue of 0.953 because it included bootstrap uncertainty and transient changes in size structure. The 18-DHW dose-response sensitivity scenario was less severe than the observed endpoint because its fitted mortality was 95.5%, rather than the 97.8--100% mortality reported from the Florida Keys and Dry Tortugas [@manzello2025]. In these recruitment-free projections, recurrence of the observed Florida mortality drove abundance below the quasi-extinction threshold even at the longest return interval. The scenarios do not forecast recovery across the Caribbean.

Complete-matrix uncertainty gave similar baseline projections for the original size composition: effective lambda was 0.936 and 80.7% of simulations fell below 10% of initial abundance at year 50 (Online Resource 1, Table S5). In the paired comparison using all 2,000 bootstrap draws, 82.5% of lambda-only simulations were below the year-50 threshold. Initial size composition had a larger effect: with full matrices and no heatwaves, the fraction below the threshold ranged from 23.2% for large-colony-heavy populations to 90.8% for small-colony-heavy populations. Under observed Florida mortality, at least 99.85% of simulations were below the threshold across all four starting compositions and prescribed return intervals.

## Discussion

Restoration programs need to know whether adding colonies will rebuild living coral cover and sustain it after planting. Larger *A. palmata* colonies generally survived more often, but survivors frequently lost tissue, and persistence in the largest class contributed most to our model without sexual recruitment. Earlier work established the importance of large colonies and the consequences of fragmentation [@lirman2000; @vardi2012]. Our synthesis quantifies tissue loss among survivors and connects size-dependent survival, growth, and shrinkage in a common model with uncertainty. A small outplant that grows rapidly but dies and a large outplant that survives while losing tissue contribute differently to the planted cohort. Trials that follow survival and retained live area across the original cohort can test which planting treatments improve these outcomes. Measuring reproductive output and recovery of habitat structure requires additional observations [@croquer2026]. Table 1 distinguishes trials of colony care and outplant size from measurements of reproduction, heat tolerance, and genotype retention.

### Colony size connects survival to persistence

The demographic contribution of a surviving colony depends on the size it retains. Size-structured theory captures this distinction by separating survival from growth and movement to smaller classes [@hughes1984; @hughesjackson1985]. In our matrix, large-colony stasis had the greatest elasticity, agreeing with the northern Florida Keys model of Vardi et al. [-@vardi2012] and with Lirman's [-@lirman2000] observations of persistence, reproduction, and fragment production in large *A. palmata* colonies. Agreement with Vardi is partly expected because Florida Keys monitoring also dominates our largest-size-class data. Survival did not ensure retention of colony size: tissue loss occurred in 44.4% of SC5 growth records, and 11.4% of surviving colonies moved into smaller classes from SC5 in the annualized transitions. Disease and corallivore stress on large colonies in St. John [@groberdunsmore2006] provide a further reason to distinguish whole-colony mortality from partial loss of live tissue. Live-area loss need not imply an equivalent loss of physical habitat, however, because the standing skeleton may remain after tissue dies. Our area records do not measure skeletal persistence or three-dimensional complexity.

First fertility and widespread reproductive maturity are different demographic milestones. In Panama, Soong and Lang [-@soonglang1992] recorded a fertile *A. palmata* colony measuring 16 × 8 cm, but estimated a puberty size of 1,600 cm² from size-ranked observations of fertility. Their area estimate summed the living surface area of branches and the colony base, unlike the area measures and live-area index pooled here. These observations support increasing reproductive potential with size, but cannot assign fertility directly to our classes. Neither growth into SC5 nor retrogression out of it establishes a change in reproductive status.

For any *A. palmata* restoration effort, the size pattern identifies a comparison worth testing: whether the survival advantage of larger outplants compensates for their slower proportional growth. Relative growth declined with size and changed curvature near 35 cm², whereas survival generally increased over a much larger size range. These observations support following initial-size treatments through both mortality and subsequent growth, rather than selecting a planting size from the curvature point alone. Published studies illustrate why the outcomes need to be measured together. Larger initial fragments survived more often than smaller fragments in long-term monitoring [@forrester2013]. In a single-genotype experiment, however, smaller fragments that survived had a larger increase in live-area index than larger survivors, without a detected survival difference between initial sizes [@pausch2018]. Trials comparing initial sizes within shared genotypes and sites could identify which sizes retain the most living area across the original planted cohort. Our pooled observations identify that question but cannot establish an optimal size across genotypes and sites.

### Survival estimates depend on study setting and disturbance history

An annual survival estimate is useful for restoration planning only in relation to the setting it represents. The 38.0–95.8% prediction interval shows why the pooled mean cannot serve as a single expected survival rate for a new study setting. Grouping intervals by regional disturbance-screening windows did not remove those differences: the small group without a catalogue match came from a restricted set of studies and years, rather than matched colonies at the same sites. Its lower observed survival in some classes therefore provides no evidence that acute disturbance protected large colonies. The event catalogue and classification rules make this comparison reproducible (Online Resource 1, Tables S2 and S3), but regional overlap does not establish exposure at an individual reef. Selecting a survival rate requires information on site, survey period, propagation and handling history, and observed mortality events, beyond a disturbed or undisturbed label.

Storm-driven simulations [@lirman2003] and the 2023 Florida observations [@manzello2025] address mortality pulses that interrupt between-event demography, rather than differences among pooled exposure categories. Manzello et al. reported 97.8–100% mortality of *A. palmata* in the Florida Keys and Dry Tortugas. Applying that mortality to our matrix reduced effective $\lambda$ even when events recurred only once every 50 years. The scenario does not estimate future heatwave frequency. It shows that the compiled monitored rates, including fragmentation, did not compensate for these mortality pulses in our recruitment-free projections. Outplant evaluations conducted over short intervals can characterize establishment; they cannot establish persistence through disturbances they did not observe.

Selecting stock for heat resistance requires evidence that an early advantage persists through later demographic stages. Karp et al. [-@karp2025] found higher acute heat tolerance in *A. palmata* hosting *Durusdinium* than in colonies hosting *Symbiodinium*. Miller et al. [-@miller2024] documented lower bleaching among sexually propagated juveniles in a multi-species Caribbean comparison. In their Mexican cohort of 19 sexually propagated *A. palmata* older than ten years, however, no colony retained healthy coloration and 32% had died by the survey. These observations motivate trials across host genotypes or families, symbiont associations, and sites, with follow-up through recovery and reproductive maturity. Acute assays and juvenile bleaching scores alone cannot establish lifetime resilience. Our pooled size relationships likewise cannot identify heat-resistant stock or isolate effects of sexual origin from age and rearing history.

Geographic coverage and differences in measurement also limit the transfer of pooled rates to a particular restoration site. Florida Keys studies supplied 85.5% of the individual-level survival inventory. Colonies outside Florida, especially the largest colonies, were poorly represented; studies also differed in mortality definitions and monitoring intervals. In the Neely records, LAI combined colony dimensions with live-tissue proportion, sometimes across several tissue isolates, and was not interchangeable with age or intact planar area. Site-level responses, including the collapse at Marker 3 during 2014 bleaching, and known or suspected clonality also limited the independent information carried by colony counts. Site, year, and colony diagnostic models addressed repeated observations, but neither the pooled curves nor study-level bootstrap resolved genotype-specific or site-specific disturbance responses. Most matrix bootstrap estimates remained below replacement under alternative resampling, although the primary bootstrap interval extended just above one. The separate Vardi exclusions showed that fragment production contributed more to the change in $\lambda$ than removal of its survival observations. Applying these pooled rates locally therefore requires checking the measurement convention, source composition, and disturbance history against the target site.

Scaling from local colonies to connected populations would require data on fecundity, fertilization, larval dispersal, settlement, and early survival. A spatially explicit metapopulation model could test whether larvae arriving from surviving reefs replace colonies lost locally. Our matrix adds neither locally produced sexual recruits nor recruits arriving from other reefs, so it cannot estimate that rescue or regional extinction risk.

Safeguarding genetic material is a separate conservation outcome from maintaining colonies on reefs. Muller et al. [-@muller2026] describe how restoration infrastructure, rescue efforts, and living gene banks preserved genetic richness during the 2023 Florida losses. Inventories of genotypes across reef sites and backup facilities could identify holdings without redundancy or with shared exposure. Estimating the benefit of diversification or rescue would require spatial-risk and intervention data absent from our model. Genotype retention and rescue survival therefore require their own evaluation rather than being inferred from reef-colony $\lambda$.

### Retaining colonies and rebuilding populations require different actions

Care of existing wild colonies and restoration outplanting are different ways to retain living coral on reefs. Persistence in the largest live-area class strongly influenced the modeled growth rate, giving a reason to test whether care reduces mortality or tissue loss in that class. Potential interventions include predator removal, removal of encroaching organisms, lesion treatment or amputation, probiotics, shading or supplemental feeding during bleaching, and stabilization after storm damage. These are candidates for controlled comparisons, not benefits established by this synthesis. Evidence also needs to match the diagnosed problem: snail removal reduced feeding scars on *A. palmata* [@williams2014snails], whereas experimental neighbors differed in their effects on fragment survival and growth [@ramosromero2026]. Replicated site blocks could compare wild-colony care, restoration outplanting, their combination, and no added intervention. Recording colony survival and total live area before and after treatment would test whether an action preserves more coral than the control. Our sensitivity analysis identifies rates that matter to the model; only those treatment comparisons could establish which actions improve them.

Planted cohorts contribute through both survival and subsequent size change. Following every original outplant, with repeat area measurements and dead colonies counted as zero, would show whether rapid growth among survivors compensates for losses elsewhere in the cohort [@madin2025]. Trials could compare clonal sizes within shared genotypes and sites, then report retained cohort area and the fraction reaching a target live-area class. The seven propagation and observation groups defined in Methods cannot be treated as interchangeable planting methods. Nursery-held fragments have not yet faced reef outplanting, and naturally occurring fragments are not deliberate plantings. The NOAA fragment sample was selected among living endpoints, so it cannot estimate starting-cohort survival; a prospective study of natural fragments could do so. Kuffner's block-mounted nursery fragments [@kuffner2020] likewise do not test the benefit of reattaching broken colonies. Such a test would compare survival and growth of comparable broken fragments with and without reattachment. For sexual propagation, the fraction of outplants surviving their first year on a reef uses the number planted as its denominator. Survival from fertilization through larval rearing and nursery production uses earlier starting numbers and measures additional losses [@chamberland2015; @mendozaquiroz2023]. Reporting those stages separately would make comparisons interpretable; recording costs and production time alongside them would permit comparisons of cost-effectiveness.

Sexual reproduction adds a further requirement for rebuilding populations. Some sexually propagated *A. palmata* are known to have spawned [@mendozaquiroz2023], and mixed-genotype planting groups can support fertilization [@baums2019]. Adding many fragments of one clone increases colony number without adding mating partners of different genotypes. Observing spawning also leaves open whether fertilized larvae settle and survive as new colonies. Trials of planting arrangements could therefore follow identified genotypes through fertility, fertilization, settlement, and recruit survival, using parentage where feasible. Croquer et al. [-@croquer2026] call survival of outplants to reproductive maturity effective return: a reproductive endpoint, rather than a financial return on restoration investment. Demonstrating replacement of lost colonies through those outplants' offspring requires recruitment observations absent from our matrix. Recovery of habitat structure likewise requires measurements beyond live area.

The projections make the demographic shortfall explicit. Even with fragmentation, the point estimate was below replacement, although the bootstrap interval included one. Florida-scale heatwave mortality drove all 500 primary observed-endpoint simulations below 10% of initial abundance at year 50. These are severe conditional outcomes, not species-extinction probabilities: sexual recruitment from local or other reefs was absent, and future heatwave frequency was not estimated. They give urgency to testing whether care and planting improve retention through disturbances, whether heat resistance persists to reproductive maturity, and whether surviving genotypes produce recruits. Safeguarding genetic material can preserve options during that work [@muller2026], but it is not itself recovery of a self-sustaining reef population.
