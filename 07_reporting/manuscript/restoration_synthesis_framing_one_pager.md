# Published evidence for *Acropora palmata* restoration decisions

Our 18-study synthesis shows why survival alone is an incomplete restoration endpoint: tissue was lost in 41.7% of 4,301 near-annual growth records from surviving natural colonies in two studies. Persistence of large colonies most influenced our recruitment-free model, consistent with earlier work [1]. These findings identify outcomes and tests, not a best outplant size or husbandry treatment.

## Priorities for restoration research and action

**Compare retaining existing colonies with producing replacements.** Our tissue-loss results motivate testing targeted care alongside additional planting. A published snail-removal experiment reduced feeding scars [2], providing a specific intervention to evaluate where predation is documented. Compare retained tissue and labor costs, without interpreting model influence as proof that maintenance is cost-effective.

**Compare planting choices using complete cohorts.** Keep natural fragments, clonal outplants, and sexual recruits separate. Build on existing size experiments [3] by comparing sizes within genotypes and sites, following every original outplant. Record production losses and nursery time separately: strong post-outplant survival can follow substantial earlier mortality [4]. Our synthesis does not rank propagation methods.

**Test reproductive neighborhoods.** Genetic guidance supports mixed-genotype groups [5]. Test spacing and genotype composition using fertilization and subsequent recruitment, rather than colony counts or spawning alone. This addresses a process absent from our model; it does not follow from its largest-class result or establish a universal planting distance.

**Validate heat resilience through maturation.** Test host genotype, symbionts, colony age, and site together. Higher acute heat tolerance with *Durusdinium* [6] and lower bleaching among sexually propagated juveniles [7] identify candidates for trials, not proven lifetime resilience. Our pooled size patterns cannot establish a heat-resistant stock type.

**Evaluate disturbance preparedness across the restoration network.** Our Florida heatwave scenarios [8] motivate assessing shared exposure across sites, maintaining genetic backups, and testing emergency-response plans. Rescue networks and gene banks have a separately documented conservation role [9]. The scenarios neither forecast return periods nor quantify the benefit of relocation or gene banking.

**Match local interventions to observed losses.** Distinguish attachment failure, predation, and overgrowth before choosing treatments. A Cuban field experiment found that effects of neighboring benthic organisms depended on their identity and site [10]. Our aggregated tissue-loss records cannot identify which cause to treat; local controlled comparisons are needed.

## Evaluate strategies against their intended outcomes

Compare maintenance, clonal planting, sexual propagation, and combinations against matched controls and explicit goals [11]. Report living area across the original planted cohort and full production and maintenance costs. Genotype retention, reproductive output, natural recruitment, and three-dimensional habitat recovery require separate measurements. These priorities extend the demographic questions; they are not intervention effects estimated by our synthesis.
