# Demographic evidence and restoration priorities for *Acropora palmata*

Restoring *Acropora palmata* requires understanding whether colonies survive long enough, and retain enough living tissue, to contribute to recovery. Our synthesis brings those outcomes together across 18 studies. Its contribution is to quantify demographic patterns and identify restoration comparisons that could improve them, rather than prescribe a single planting method.

## What published studies already establish

Large-colony persistence was already important in Vardi et al.’s Florida Keys population model [1]. Field studies also documented size-dependent outplant survival [12], recovery after fragmentation, and delayed reproduction following severe damage [13]. Size experiments have produced context-dependent results: Pausch et al. found more tissue production by smaller fragments, without a detected survival difference, in a single-genotype comparison [3]. These studies established the importance of colony size and tissue retention before our synthesis.

Restoration can produce reproductive colonies. Sexually propagated *A. palmata* have survived to fertility and spawning, although high early production losses precede the outplanted cohort [4]. Genetic guidance emphasizes maintaining diversity and mixed-genotype groups [5]. More recently, the 2023 Florida heatwave demonstrated the vulnerability of reef populations [8], while rescue networks and gene banks helped safeguard genetic material [9]. These are distinct achievements: producing colonies, achieving reproduction, and preserving genotypes do not individually demonstrate self-sustaining reef recovery.

## What our synthesis adds and why it matters

**Survival can conceal substantial loss of living coral.** Tissue loss occurred in 41.7% of 4,301 near-annual growth records from surviving natural colonies in two studies. Many losses did not move colonies into a smaller size class. Quantifying this distinction shows why survival alone can overstate demographic performance. Repeated live-area measurements can reveal losses that a count of surviving outplants misses.

**A survival benchmark changes with the evidence included.** Pooled annual survival was 78.7%, but its 95% prediction interval spanned 37.4–95.8%. In a separate, record-weighted source comparison, excluding natural fragments lowered observed survival from 81.2% to 60.0%. These are different estimators, not a treatment effect. Their practical message is that naturally generated fragments cannot silently stand in for planted stock when programs set expectations or compare performance.

**The combined rates describe persistence without full replacement.** Larger colonies generally survived more often, while proportional growth declined with size. Combining survival and live-area transitions gave annual population growth, lambda, of 0.941 (95% bootstrap interval 0.892–0.982), below the replacement value of one. The model includes asexual fragmentation but excludes sexual recruitment. Largest-class persistence contributed most to elasticity, reinforcing Vardi’s result [1]; some monitoring information overlaps. The addition is an integrated estimate with uncertainty that connects survival to growth and shrinkage. Model influence identifies a consequential outcome, not the most cost-effective intervention.

Applying the observed 2023 Florida mortality at prescribed intervals further reduced effective growth, even with one event every 50 years. This stress test shows why good performance between events is insufficient evidence of long-term persistence; it does not forecast heatwave frequency. Florida Keys records supplied 84.7% of individual survival observations, so local validation remains essential. Main Figs. 1–4 and Online Resource 1 Figs. S3, S5 and S6 document these synthesis results.

## Restoration research and action priorities

**1 Compare care of existing colonies with additional planting.** In replicated site blocks, compare targeted care, planting, their combination, and unchanged practice. Measure changes in retained live area and genotype retention alongside costs. Snail removal reduced feeding scars in a published experiment [2], but neither that result nor our elasticity analysis establishes that maintenance generally outperforms planting.

**2 Follow complete production and planted cohorts.** Compare clonal outplant sizes within shared genotypes and sites; retain family, age, and rearing history for sexual cohorts. Measure living area across every original outplant, not growth among survivors alone. Include nursery losses, production time, and maintenance costs [3,4]. Our pooled curves identify a size-related trade-off, not an optimal planting size.

**3 Test whether planting arrangements support recruitment.** Compare arrangements of genetically identified outplants with comparable initial size and source composition [5]. Follow survival to reproductive condition, spawning overlap, fertilization, settlement, and recruit survival. Use parentage where feasible. Distinguish arrangement effects from the number of reproductive genotypes present. Our recruitment-free matrix cannot prescribe spacing or estimate reproductive return.

**4 Test heat resilience through maturation and recovery.** Acute tolerance associated with *Durusdinium* [6] and lower bleaching among sexually propagated juveniles in a multi-species study [7] motivate replicated comparisons across host genotypes or families, symbionts, and sites. Record exposure and rearing history, distinguish manipulations from existing associations, and follow tissue recovery and reproduction into adulthood. Early resistance does not establish lifetime resilience.

**5 Prepare for losses shared across sites.** Map genotype holdings, environmental exposure, and backup facilities to identify holdings without redundancy. Establish rescue responsibilities, capacity, and response triggers before heatwaves. Evaluate genotype retention, rescue survival, and costs afterward [8,9]. Our scenarios motivate preparedness but cannot quantify the benefit of geographic diversification, relocation, or gene banking.

**6 Target the cause and life stage of loss.** Distinguish attachment failure, disappearance, confirmed death, predation, disease signs, and overgrowth before choosing interventions. Compare diagnosed treatments with controls and track both survival and retained tissue. Neighbor effects vary by organism and site [10], so blanket clearing is not justified. Treatments for established fragments need separate tests at settlement and early recruit stages.

## Evaluate strategies against explicit recovery goals

Compare maintenance, clonal planting, sexual propagation, and combinations over common intervals with controls and full costs [11]. Retained living area, distinct genotypes, reproduction, and natural recruitment measure different outcomes. Habitat recovery additionally requires skeletal complexity and associated communities. Measured intervention effects and recruitment could extend the model to compare strategies; the present synthesis cannot rank them.
