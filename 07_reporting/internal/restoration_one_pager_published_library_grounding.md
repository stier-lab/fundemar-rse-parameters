## Consequential changes to the framing

The previous note proposed a size comparison without adequately acknowledging published size experiments. The revision contrasts Forrester and Pausch rather than implying that planting size is unstudied. It also credits existing attachment experiments, separates occurrence models from performance tests, and recognizes prior evidence for reproductive return. The proposed extension concerns repeatability across genotypes and sites plus retained live area and production costs; it is not labelled a demonstrated optimum or an unprecedented experiment.

## Added published arguments about restoration

- Reference 12: Kuffner et al. (2020), Reestablishing a stepping-stone population of the threatened elkhorn coral Acropora palmata to aid regional recovery. Endangered Species Research 43:461–473, DOI 10.3354/esr01083. Read p. 461, abstract, in `literature/pdfs/data_studies/Kuffner_etal_2020.pdf`. Assisted migration and a sexually reproducing stepping-stone population are proposed paths to regional recovery, not demonstrated regional rescue. The 2018–2019 performance results do not establish persistence through the 2023 heatwave.
- Reference 13: Muller et al. (2026), Success of restoration strategies in preventing extirpation of 2 critically endangered coral species. Conservation Biology 40:e70168, DOI 10.1111/cobi.70168. Read pp. 1–3 in `literature/pdfs/restoration/Muller_etal_2025_restoration_prevents_extirpation.pdf`. Authors credit the restoration community, infrastructure, living gene banks and emergency response with preventing regional extirpation and preserving genetic richness. They also document losses in outplanted, wild and ocean-nursery populations; the argument is not that outplanting made reef colonies heat-proof. The local advance-online PDF is dated 2025. Publisher's current how-to-cite record gives 2026, volume 40; checked https://conbio.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cobi.70168 on 19 October 2026. The note uses the publisher's issue citation without altering the library PDF.
- Reference 14: Boström-Einarsson et al. (2020), Coral restoration—A systematic review of current methods, successes, failures and future directions. PLoS ONE 15:e0226631, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0226631. Read pp. 1–2 in `literature/pdfs/restoration/BostromEinarsson_etal_2020_coral_restoration_review.pdf`. The review identifies short follow-up and small project footprints, asks for clear goals and better monitoring, and cautions against substituting restoration for climate action. This is a multi-species global review, not A. palmata-specific experimental evidence.
- Reference 10 is also used for its explicit argument about goal definition, controlled designs and long-term ecosystem outcomes. These published positions are attributed to their authors; no claim of unanimity across the library is made. The demographic matrix cannot test the value of gene banking or demonstrate ecosystem recovery.

## Additional distinctions checked on the second pass

- **Colonies versus genetic individuals:** Reference 15, Baums, Miller and Hellberg (2006), Geographic variation in clonal structure in a reef-building Caribbean coral, Acropora palmata. Ecological Monographs 76:503–519. Read pp. 503–504 in `literature/pdfs/data_studies/Baums_etal_2006_clonal_structure.pdf`. The study distinguishes ramets from genets using microsatellites across 26 reefs. Increasing the number of clonal fragments is not equivalent to adding distinct genotypes; it can nevertheless preserve or propagate existing genets. Do not imply that clonality has no conservation value.
- **Gamete production versus reproductive success:** Reference 16, Baums et al. (2013), Genotypic variation influences reproductive success and thermal stress tolerance in the reef building coral, Acropora palmata. Coral Reefs 32:703–717, DOI 10.1007/s00338-013-1012-6. Read pp. 703–704 in `/Users/adrianstier/literature-db/pdfs/Baums_2013_GenotypicVariationInfluencesReproductiveSuccess__005291.pdf`. Controlled crosses show variation in fertilization and gamete compatibility. Fertility and spawning do not establish natural recruitment, realized connectivity, or population replacement. These later stages require their own observations.
- **Release-stage survival versus production-to-reef yield:** Rechecked Mendoza Quiroz et al. (2023), pp. 1–2. High early ex-situ mortality and low later mortality refer to different stages. A surviving outplanted subset cannot supply survival from fertilization or settlement. The revision retains separate denominators for production losses and the original planted cohort, without ranking sexual versus clonal propagation.
- **Productive site versus thermal refuge:** Rechecked Kuffner et al. (2020), p. 461: performance observations were May 2018–October 2019. Manzello et al. (2025), p. 361, records 97.8–100% mortality in the Florida Keys and Dry Tortugas during 2023. This contrasts study periods and regional outcomes; it does not establish that the exact Kuffner experimental colonies were resurveyed in 2023 or that assisted migration is ineffective. The note must not imply either.
- **Demographic influence versus achievable intervention benefit:** Vardi et al. (2012) identifies influential transitions, but a large elasticity is not a treatment effect, a prediction of a feasible management-induced change, or a benefit-cost analysis. Our recruitment-free matrix does not rank interventions or represent sexual population recovery.
- **Growth among survivors versus the retained planted cohort:** Tracking living area across every original outplant prevents growth among a surviving subset from being mistaken for recovery of the whole cohort. Nursery time, production mortality, and costs remain additional measurements, not outputs of the current synthesis.
- **Living area versus habitat:** Live planar area measures neither standing skeletal persistence nor three-dimensional structure, associated communities or ecosystem function. Separate measurements are needed before making habitat-recovery claims.
